### Description ###
A small tabletop Stirling engine converts thermal energy from a heat source into continuous mechanical rotation of a flywheel by cyclically heating and cooling a trapped gas.

### Equipment ###
* Tabletop Stirling engine model (gamma or beta configuration)
* Heat source (alcohol lamp with denatured alcohol, or a mug of hot water for low-temperature-differential models)
* Matches or lighter
* Heat-resistant mat

### Instruction ###
* Initial setup: Place the Stirling engine on a flat, heat-resistant surface. Ensure the flywheel spins freely by hand without friction or obstruction.
* Applying heat: Light the alcohol lamp and position it directly beneath the hot end (displacer cylinder) of the engine, or place a low-temperature engine on top of a mug of hot water. Wait 30 to 60 seconds for the cylinder wall to warm up.
* Starting the cycle: Give the flywheel a gentle spin by hand to initiate the thermodynamic cycle. Once started, the engine will accelerate and run continuously on its own.
* Stopping the engine: Extinguish the flame or remove the heat source. Observe the flywheel gradually slowing down and stopping as the temperature difference between the hot and cold ends equalizes.

### Physics ###
A Stirling engine operates as a closed-cycle external combustion heat engine using a fixed amount of trapped air as its working fluid. Heat from the flame enters one end of the cylinder, causing the trapped air to warm up and expand. This expansion pushes the power piston outward, turning the flywheel. The flywheel's momentum then drives a secondary displacer piston that shuttles the hot air toward the cooler end of the cylinder, where it rejects heat to the surrounding room air and contracts. The lower pressure allows the flywheel to push the power piston back inward, repeating the four-step cycle (isothermal expansion, isochoric cooling, isothermal compression, and isochoric heating) as long as a temperature difference is maintained.

### Concepts ###
* Heat engine: a system that converts thermal energy into mechanical work by operating between a high-temperature heat source and a low-temperature heat sink.
* Second Law of Thermodynamics: heat naturally flows from a hotter region to a colder region, and no cyclic heat engine can convert 100% of absorbed heat into mechanical work without rejecting some waste heat.
* Stirling cycle: a closed regenerative thermodynamic cycle consisting of two isothermal (constant temperature) processes and two isochoric (constant volume) processes.
